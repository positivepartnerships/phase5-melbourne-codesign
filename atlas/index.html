§ 01 Preamble

A working map, not a compliance checklist.

Why this atlas exists, what it is for, and what kind of artefact it is.

What it is A single-file webpage you can read, print, or extend. Not a content library. Not a curriculum. A scaffold for Phase 5 design decisions, anchored in the documents PP is funded against.

What it is not A summary of the source documents. The PDFs are linked beneath each anchor card — read them, do not let a derivative replace them.

Seven Commonwealth documents plus three AutismCRC best-practice guidelines form the foundation Phase 5 must design against. Educators carry the DDA and DSE. Leaders carry the APSP and NCCD. Teachers carry the APST and Curriculum v9. Families carry rights as associates. And everyone is now held within the National Autism Strategy 2025–31 — moving the conversation from compliance to recognition, belonging and co-design.

Anchor documents across the top of the matrix. Eight content pillars down the side. Cells show where each document binds, suggests or speaks into each pillar. Each pillar opens out into a working brief — what the documents say, what they mean in practice, what the workshop should equip people to do.

§ 04 The Pillars

Eight content pillars, read in conversation.

Each pillar opens with one sentence — the design provocation — and a working brief for content. Star a pillar to keep it; favourites persist locally on this device.

Foreground for:
Pillar 01 · Foundations

Rights, Standards & the new ECEC frontier

What educators, families and ECEC providers operate within — and what changes on 1 August 2026.

Why this is first

Every other pillar sits on the DDA's twin questions: are opportunities comparable? and has the duty to adjust been honoured? The DSE 2005 was the Commonwealth's answer for education. The 2025 Amendment extends that answer to early childhood services on 1 August 2026 — a workforce that has never been bound by the Standards before.

Phase 5 implication: PP has a narrow window to build ECEC-facing content. The educators reached for the last twenty years were teachers; the educators about to need the same workshops are ECEC professionals who have never sat under the DSE.

What workshops must equip people to do

  • Read the phrase “on the same basis” (DSE s 2.2) as a working test, not legal jargon — are the opportunities and choices comparable?
  • Distinguish direct from indirect discrimination (DDA ss 5–6) — most education complaints turn on the second.
  • Recognise imputed disability (DDA s 4(k)): the duty arises before a formal diagnosis.
  • Know that associates — parents, carers, siblings — are protected in their own right (DDA s 4, DSE Part 9).
  • For ECEC providers: understand that from 1 August 2026 the Standards apply to your service, your children and any associate seeking enrolment.
  • Read the new Statement of Rights boxes the 2025 Amendment inserts at the head of Parts 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 — these are designed to make the rights legible to the people they belong to.

“An education provider treats a student with a disability on the same basis as a student without a disability if the provider makes any decisions about admission or enrolment on the basis that reasonable adjustments will be provided.” DSE 2005, s 2.2(2)

Where the workshop content has historically been weak

Two failure modes are common: treating the DSE as a paperwork exercise (a register of forms), and the “diagnosis-first” misconception (believing a formal autism diagnosis is required before adjustments are due). Both can be unlearned in a one-hour workshop. Both will recur if Phase 5 doesn't name them.

Pillar 02 · Consultation

Consultation as practice, not paperwork.

The most-repeated obligation across every framework — and the one done most thinly in practice.

Why this is the load-bearing wall

The duty to consult repeats under different headings across the anchors. DSE writes it five times (s 3.5; Parts 4.2(3), 5.2(2), 6.2(2), 7.2(5)/(6)). NCCD names it as Evidence Area 1. APST 7.3. NAS Co-design. Same duty, different labels.

Whoever consults well — at enrolment, in adjustment review, around an incident, at transition — has the single most useful skill in this framework.

The AutismCRC guidelines deepen this. The Supporting Children Guideline separates assent (Rec 11 — child's right to say no, words or otherwise) from informed consent (Rec 12 — parent's right to enough information to choose). The Assessment Guideline's first principle is client- and family-centred. Phase 5 consultation literacy should teach both.

What real consultation looks like

  • Begins with the student themselves where possible (DSE s 3.5 names the student first, the associate second).
  • Recognises AAC, gesture, drawing and other modes of communication — non-speaking students are not outside the duty.
  • Asks the question prescribed by s 3.5(c): is there any other adjustment that would be less disruptive and intrusive and no less beneficial? This is the student's right to redirect.
  • Captures what was said, by whom, when — for the NCCD evidence base and for the student's record.
  • Repeats. DSE s 5.2(3) requires the process to recur as needs change.

Design provocation: What would it look like to name “consultation literacy” as a single transferable skill across every PP workshop, taught explicitly with practice and assessment? At present it is implicit in many workshops and absent from the rest.

The 2025 Amendment's quiet shift

The Amendment's new Statement of Rights boxes reframe each Part as the student's entitlement, not the provider's obligation. That changes the script of a consultation. Reproduce the boxes visibly in workshop content so educators and families read the same words.

Pillar 03 · Adjustments

Reasonable adjustments in real classrooms.

From legal concept to daily practice, and the language teachers need to share with families.

The working definition

DSE s 3.3 defines an adjustment as a measure that helps a student participate on the same basis. DSE s 3.4 makes it reasonable when it “balances the interests of all parties.” The s 3.4(2) factors — student's disability, student's views, effect on the student, effect on others, costs and benefits — are the terms of a conversation, not a checklist.

NCCD carries the same concept: four levels (QDTP, Supplementary, Substantial, Extensive) and four categories (Physical, Cognitive, Sensory, Social/Emotional). Most Autistic students recorded under Cognitive and Social/Emotional — often both.

What is an adjustment not?

Not a modification of the curriculum standard. DSE s 3.4(3) preserves academic integrity. The 2025 Amendment rewrites s 6.2(1) to make this sharper: the course must be designed so a student with disability can participate in learning experiences and assessment and certification on the same basis. Adjustments operate on v9; they don't substitute for it.

What workshops should equip people to do

  • Distinguish QDTP (universal practice, no NCCD recording above QDTP) from supplementary, substantial and extensive.
  • Use a working vocabulary of sensory adjustments — lighting, sound, transitions, predictable routines, regulated retreat spaces — without medicalising them.
  • Use a working vocabulary of communication adjustments — AAC, visual supports, processing time, pre-teaching of vocabulary, written checking.
  • Use a working vocabulary of cognitive load adjustments — chunking, scaffolds, organisers, executive function supports.
  • Document the adjustment, the consultation that informed it, and the review interval — for NCCD, for the student's record, and for the consultation history that follows the student into the next setting.

“Compliance with the measures is not required if the legal obligation can be met in other ways.” DSE 2005, Introduction

How the AutismCRC guidelines sharpen this

SAC's 84 Recommendations sit across nine focal areas — communication, sensory, cognitive, social-emotional, motor, academic, daily activities, family wellbeing, accessible environments. Where DSE says "reasonable adjustment" in the abstract, SAC names what is being adjusted.

S&S 2024's F-Words — Fitness, Functioning, Friends, Family, Fun, Future — give a single child-focused vocabulary for what an adjustment is for. Mapping DSE adjustments and NCCD levels through F-Words gives teachers and families a shared language they currently lack.

Pillar 04 · Curriculum

Curriculum access on the same basis.

What “same basis” means across F–10 and senior secondary, when the curriculum is held constant.

The two halves of the same right

Curriculum access has two halves — delivery (the learning experiences) and assessment and certification. DSE s 6.2 covered the first; the 2025 Amendment rewrites s 6.2(1) to make both explicit, closing a long-standing ambiguity that had let providers adjust pedagogy without adjusting assessment.

v9 as inclusion architecture

v9 isn't an obstacle to inclusion. The seven General Capabilities are the natural levers — Personal and Social Capability especially, sitting across every learning area with self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and social management. HPE carries identity, respectful relationships and emotional regulation.

Language note: This atlas avoids “social skills.” The Curriculum's framing is “social awareness” and “social management”; Autistic-led research prefers “social interaction” / “social communication.” A small change with a large message: two-way capacity, not unilateral deficit.

What workshops should equip people to do

  • Use the difference between adjustment, differentiation and modification as a precise instrument — they are not synonyms in NCCD or DSE.
  • Design assessment access without diluting the assessed construct (extra time, alternative formats, oral response, scribe, breaks, environment).
  • Map adjustments to General Capabilities — Personal and Social is the most generative.
  • Translate v9 architecture across the jurisdictional implementations (VIC v2.0, NSW K–10 syllabuses, etc.).
  • Plan with Universal Design for Learning principles — multiple means of engagement, representation, action and expression — knowing they sit cleanly within v9's framing.

Pillar 05 · Belonging

Belonging, safety & anti-bullying.

The one DSE Part that allows no unjustifiable hardship exception — and the area where outcomes for Autistic students are weakest.

The unyielding standard

DSE Part 8 is unique: the s 10.2 unjustifiable hardship exception doesn't apply. A provider cannot tell a court that preventing harassment was too expensive or too difficult. The duty is to have strategies and programs in place (s 8.3), extended to students with associates (Guidance Notes §9).

What the data tells Phase 5

68.9% of Autistic 5–20 year-olds experience difficulty at their place of learning. Disproportionate bullying, harassment and educational exclusion. 2.5× depression rate in Autistic adults. These are the result of belonging not being designed for.

“Inclusion means systemic, societal and attitudinal change across all four outcome areas of the Strategy.” National Autism Strategy 2025–2031, p.7

What workshops should equip people to do

  • Treat anti-bullying as a primary curriculum object, not an after-incident response.
  • Recognise that masking — the work of camouflaging Autistic traits to fit a neurotypical environment — is itself a safety signal that belonging is not being designed for (NAS Statement on Autism).
  • Design peer culture interventions explicitly: neurodiversity literacy across all students, not just Autistic students.
  • Cover the schoolyard, structured-time and online environments — Part 8 does not stop at the classroom door.
  • Recognise that associates (siblings, parents) are also captured by Part 8 — sibling harassment based on a brother or sister's disability is the same duty.
  • Build feedback mechanisms students will actually use — many existing complaint pathways are inaccessible by design.

Phase 5 design provocation: Make Pillar 5 a workshop in its own right. Treating anti-bullying as a sub-bullet within a generic inclusion workshop has reliably under-delivered for twenty years. The data is clear; the Standard is unyielding; the design choice follows.

What the AutismCRC guidelines add

SAC Section 5 places safeguarding at the heart of practice: safe environment, adverse-effects management, feedback pathways, rights protection. Rec 5: supports honour childhood. Rec 7: ethical practice protects child and family rights. Read with DSE Part 8, anti-bullying stops being reactive policy and becomes a positive design parameter.

Pillar 06 · Leadership

Whole-school leadership for inclusion.

The layer historically thinnest in PP's offering — and the one without which the rest decays.

Why the leadership tier matters

APSP names three Leadership Requirements and five Practices — inclusion lives across every Practice when a principal lets it, and in none when they don't.

DDA s 123 puts the provider (usually the principal) in the liability seat for staff conduct, unless reasonable precautions and due diligence are shown. DSE Guidance Notes §7 specifies the precautions: induction, PD, monitoring, accessible complaints. None are teacher tasks.

The system layer: approved authorities under NCCD

NCCD adds a system-level duty. The approved authority — diocesan office, independent board, government system — is responsible for evidence-base integrity across its schools. Leadership workshops that ignore this layer leave principals under-prepared.

Phase 5 recommendation (anticipated below): Build a discrete leadership stratum — a half-day principal offering, plus a middle-leadership content stream for heads of inclusion, deputies and ECEC directors. Without it, the classroom workshops are perpetually rebuilding what leadership has not held.

What workshops should equip leaders to do

  • Build staff capability over the year — induction, scheduled PD, performance review tied to inclusion practice.
  • Design the school's complaint resolution process so it is accessible, transparent, and used.
  • Make resourcing decisions through an inclusion lens, including how the NCCD-linked loading is applied at the school level.
  • Engage the community partners — including autistic-led organisations and Autism CRC — as part of the school's culture, not as guest speakers.
  • Model rights-respecting practice visibly: the principal who reads the Statement of Rights at a staff meeting changes the school's posture in a way no policy document can.

Pillar 07 · Family Partnership

Family partnership & lived experience.

Families and carers are not stakeholders; they are rights-holders, co-experts and consultation partners.

Two rights at once

Families hold two distinct rights. As consultation partner for the student (DSE s 3.5, Part 9). And in their own person as associates under DDA s 4 — protected from discrimination based on the disability of someone they're connected to. Workshops that treat families only as a route to the child miss the second entirely.

Family workshops as rights education

Phase 5 family content should treat parents and carers as rights-bearing adults needing a working grasp of: their child's DSE rights; the consultation duty they're entitled to participate in; the NCCD process recording their child's adjustments; their own DDA protections as associates. The current sector default — parenting tips, regulation strategies, self-care — under-uses what families can do with knowledge of their position.

“Families and carers can themselves face challenges in navigating complex systems, such as healthcare and social services, and in overcoming barriers to advocating for, and securing, appropriate resources, accommodations and services.” National Autism Strategy 2025–2031, p.14

Cultural humility

NAS names First Nations, CALD and CARM families as priority cohorts. PP has historically reached an over-represented sample of English-speaking, urban families. Design from the start for cultural responsiveness — interpreters, culturally specific framings, community-led delivery — not as translation after the fact.

What family workshops should equip people to do

  • Read “on the same basis” as the right their child holds, not a phrase to fight for.
  • Prepare for, participate in, and follow up after a consultation — including non-verbally with their child.
  • Understand the four NCCD evidence elements and how the family contribution shapes the record.
  • Navigate complaint pathways within the school, with the system, and with the AHRC.
  • Recognise the associate's own protections under the DDA — and use them.
  • Connect with Autistic-led community as part of identity development, not pathology management.

The Supporting Children Guideline as a parent compass

SAC's 84 Recommendations are the most concrete account of family-partnership practice currently available. Section 2 (Goal Setting) anchors every plan in four tests — relevant, safe, desired, feasible. Section 4 (Delivery) names who, what, where, how. Rec 1 establishes shared decision making. Phase 5 family workshops should treat this as their practical companion.

Pillar 08 · Transitions

Transitions & life-stage continuity.

From early childhood entry through to post-school — every transition is a known fracture point and the DSE 2025 Amendment now reaches the earliest one.

Why transitions are a pillar in their own right

The Standards apply afresh at every new setting. A child entering preschool, a student entering Year 7, a young person leaving Year 12 — each is a new prospective student in a new institution. This is where consultation evidence is most often lost: the new setting doesn't see the old one's record; the family starts over; the student is exhausted; adjustments lapse for a term.

The ECEC frontier (1 August 2026)

From 1 August 2026 the DSE applies to ECEC. This extends the first transition into a regulated handover — the ECEC service has been consulting and adjusting, and the school inherits that history (with consent). Phase 5 has a once-in-a-generation chance to design both halves of that handover at the same time.

Phase 5 design provocation: Build a single “warm handover” protocol that spans ECEC → school, primary → secondary and secondary → post-school — the consultation history is the artefact that travels with the student, not a fresh form at every stage.

What workshops should equip people to do

  • Document consultations in a form that can travel — with family consent — into the next setting.
  • Use the early Year 7 window deliberately — it is the most under-resourced moment in many students' educational lives, and a known mental-health inflection point.
  • Plan post-school continuity early — Year 10 onwards — across the NDIS, higher education access schemes, employment supports, and post-school education.
  • Recognise that identity is also a transition: many Autistic young people are diagnosed after, not before, the school transitions that affected them.
  • Treat the family as the continuity carrier between settings — they are the only adults present at every transition.

§ 05 Recommendations

Ten recommendations for Phase 5 content, 2026–2029.

The map above is descriptive. The list below is editorial: where I think the design weight should be placed, and why, read from the documents in conversation with each other.

How to read this

These are not co-designed recommendations. They are one reader's analytical reading of the seven anchor documents, offered as a draft for AutismCRC co-authorship to test, deepen or contest.

They are not in priority order — they are in the order they sit in the logic of the framework. Recommendation 01, however, is time-bound and the most consequential.

One short sentence I keep returning to: the seven documents are already saying the same things in different vocabularies. Phase 5's job is to give educators and families a single vocabulary they can use across all seven.

  1. Pivot to ECEC by mid-2026 — this is the phase-defining shift.

    The DSE 2025 Amendment commences 1 August 2026 — 31 days into Phase 5 — extending the Standards for the first time to preschools, kindergartens, centre-based day care, family day care, OSHC, vacation care, and in-home care. That workforce has never been bound by the DSE before. Phase 5's first deliverable should be an ECEC-facing stream: educators, directors, and parents/carers of preschool-aged Autistic children.

    Where to look→DSE 25 Sch 1 items 1–20 · Service 1 direction 01 in the Brief

  2. Name “consultation literacy” as a single transferable skill across every workshop.

    The same competency is asked of every educator at every juncture — at enrolment, in adjustment review, around an incident, at NCCD evidence-gathering, at every transition. The same competency is asked of every family, every co-designer, every leader. Phase 5 should make it explicit, named, taught, practised and assessed — the named skill that links all eight pillars.

    Where to look→DSE 2005 s 3.5 + every Part · Service 1 direction 02 in the Brief

  3. Co-author with Autistic adults — visibly, in every workshop.

    The NAS was co-designed; the First Action Plan continues that mode. Phase 5 content development should mirror what the Strategy prescribes. Every workshop — educator, leader, family — should have paid Autistic co-design on the development team and Autistic facilitation or co-presentation on the delivery roster, not added as a guest segment. The content models the practice it asks of schools.

    Where to look→NAS 2025–31 Co-design principle p.21 · Brief Services 1–8 directions

  4. Bring the new Statement of Rights boxes into the room.

    The 2025 Amendment inserts a plain-language Statement of Rights at the head of DSE Parts 4 to 8. This is quietly the most powerful rhetorical change in the amendment — reframing “the provider's obligation” as “the student's right” rotates the conversation by ninety degrees. Workshop materials should reproduce the boxes verbatim, so educators and families read the same words.

    Where to look→DSE 25 ss 4.1A, 5.1A, 6.1A, 7.1A, 8.1A · Brief Service 1 rec 04 + Service 2 rec 05

  5. Build the missing leadership stratum.

    PP has historically focused on classroom teachers and families; the principal, ECEC director and middle leadership tier has been thin. Phase 5 should add a half-day principal / ECEC-director offering and a continuing middle-leadership content stream (heads of inclusion, deputies, learning support coordinators), carrying Approved Authority responsibilities under NCCD into the leadership content.

    Where to look→APSP 5 Practices · DDA s 123 · Service 3 direction 01 in the Brief

  6. Make anti-bullying a workshop in its own right.

    DSE Part 8 allows no unjustifiable hardship exception. NAS data: 68.9% of Autistic 5–20 year-olds experience difficulty at their place of learning; disproportionate bullying, harassment and educational exclusion. Sub-bullet treatment within a general inclusion workshop has under-delivered for two decades. Phase 5 should design a discrete Pillar 5 workshop — covering peer culture, masking as a safety signal, online safety, and Part 9 associate reach.

    Where to look→DSE 2005 Part 8 (no UH) · NAS pp.14–17 · Service 3 direction 02 in the Brief

  7. Make NCCD comprehensible to families.

    Most families have never heard of NCCD. Most do not know that their child's adjustments are being recorded against four levels and four categories every year, or that family voice is one of NCCD's four evidence elements. A short, accessible “NCCD for families” module converts families from passive subjects of the data collection into active partners in its accuracy.

    Where to look→NCCD Guidelines 2025 Evidence Area 1 · Service 2 direction 01 in the Brief

  8. Adopt the NAS language settings, deliberately and across the board.

    Identity-first (“Autistic person”). No functioning labels. Strengths-based. Neurodiversity-affirming. “Social communication” / “social interaction”, not “social skills”. No puzzle imagery. Audit every piece of inherited PP material against this language standard before any new workshop is delivered. The work is small; the signal is large.

    Where to look→NAS Statement on Language p.8 · NAS Statement on Autism p.9

  9. Design for intersectionality from the first sketch.

    The NAS names 12 priority cohorts. PP content has historically reached an over-represented default: urban, English-speaking, school-aged, boy-coded, verbally-fluent. Co-design panels need cohort representation; case material needs cohort range; workshop delivery needs interpreters and culturally responsive framings designed in, not bolted on. The signal of seriousness is the visible diversity of the people on the workshop development teams.

    Where to look→NAS Priority Cohorts pp.10–15 · §07 Cohorts below

  10. Build identity and mental health in — quietly, accurately.

    NAS data: 2.5× depression rate. The Autism Health Roadmap will land during Phase 5. Educators are not clinicians and shouldn't be invited to become them — but they're protective factors when they support identity, recognise masking-related distress, and know warm-handover pathways. Workshop content should equip without over-equipping.

    Where to look→NAS Outcome 4 p.18 · Service 9 direction 01 in the Brief
§ 06 Mapping to the Tender

Nine Services, eight pillars — the contract underneath the framework.

The RFT for Phase 5 (PRN. ESE25/10020, closing 3 December 2025) names nine Service Types and ten General Requirements. This section maps the eight pillars above onto those nine services, names the anchors the RFT adds beyond the original seven, and flags where the framework speaks back to the Tender.

RFT at a glance Phase 5 runs 1 July 2026 – 30 June 2029, with possible 24-month extension. Tender closes 3 Dec 2025. Free service delivery; national scope; targets and KPIs in Schedule 1 Attachment A.

Funded for Professional learning for school staff; parent/carer training; whole-school community support; First Nations & CALD outreach; an online learning & information service; stakeholder engagement; data & continuous improvement; targeted projects on request.

The RFT confirms what the policy reading already implied. The Commonwealth wants Phase 5 to be designed against the seven anchors named in §02 — the RFT specifies the DDA, the DSE 2005, the NCCD, Curriculum v9, the APST, the APSP and the NAS by name (Service 1; General Requirement 5). It adds a small number of further anchors the atlas should hold alongside them: Australia's Disability Strategy 2021–31, the Disability Royal Commission's recommendations, two Senate Inquiries, Autism CRC's research base, the forthcoming Australian Government autism microcredential, and the PWDA Co-Design Programming Overview as the prescribed co-design standard.

The Services themselves are structurally familiar — five delivery streams (educators, parents/carers, whole school, First Nations, CALD), an online and resources layer, a stakeholder & promotion layer, a data & continuous improvement layer, and an on-request targeted-projects layer. The eight pillars in §04 sit underneath every delivery stream, and almost every General Requirement.

§ 07 Priority Cohorts

Intersectionality is not a chapter.

The NAS names twelve cohorts as facing compounded barriers. Every pillar above must hold these in view — not as a postscript, but as a design parameter.

First Nations Autistic people

Underdiagnosis, racism, and the absence of culturally safe pathways. Closing the Gap priority reforms apply.

CALD & CARM communities

Cultural and linguistic responsiveness as design — not translation as afterthought.

Women, girls & gender-diverse

Late identification, mental-health-first presentations, parenting-while-Autistic stigma.

LGBTQIA+ Autistic people

Higher representation; layered stigma and barriers; safety in school settings.

Older Autistic people

Late or absent diagnosis; growing sensory and communication needs over time.

Children & young people

Disproportionate bullying, harassment, educational exclusion. Phase 5's core audience.

People with very high support needs

Risk of being spoken for; reliance on family carers; AAC and non-speaking communication.

AAC & non-speaking

Right to choice over communication device(s) and method(s). Consultation duty applies fully.

Co-occurring conditions

Most Autistic people are multiply neurodivergent and/or have co-occurring conditions.

Regional & remote

Service deserts; PP's traditional reach has been thin outside metro centres.

People in care or institutions

Group homes, child protection, justice systems — significantly higher risk of harm.

Trauma experience

Many Autistic people carry trauma from systems that were supposed to support them. Trauma-informed design throughout.

§ 08 For the AutismCRC Conversation

What this atlas is offering as a starting point.

Read as a draft for co-authorship, not a finished framework — what is here to test, deepen, contest or discard with AutismCRC and the Autistic community.

What is firm

The seven documents named here are the seven that govern. The eight pillars are derived from reading these documents in conversation — they are open to renaming, recombining, or splitting where co-design finds a sharper shape.

What is provisional

The recommendations in §05 are one reader's analytical synthesis. They are written to be argued with. Recommendation 01 — the ECEC pivot — is time-bound by the legislation; the others are negotiable in weight and framing.

What is missing

This atlas does not yet hold the voice of Autistic adults, families and young people. That is the most important gap and the most important next step. Co-design panels — paid, representative, sustained — belong before any workshop content is written.

What this should become

A living document, versioned, with the version notes visible. A workshop content map that holds across Phase 5 and the documents above. A point of reference for AutismCRC co-authorship sessions. Not the content; the map under the content.
