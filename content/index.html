§ SWOT ideation highlights

What the team named in our Phase 4 SWOT.

A working snapshot from the team’s SWOT ideation on Phase 4 content — framing what to carry into Phase 5, what to retire, what to chase, and what to be careful of.

S Strengths

What seems to be working well.

  • Shared language across audiences was seen as a real strength: parents/carers and educators getting aligned concepts and vocabulary makes later collaboration easier.
  • The planning tool / diversity wheel was described as highly valued and practical, even if the delivery around it may need updating.
  • Contextualised delivery was repeatedly highlighted: DTMs adapt examples and discussion to the local cohort, community, and participant mix rather than delivering a purely generic national script.
  • Community-building effects, especially in multicultural parent/carer workshops, were seen as a major strength. People are not just learning content; they are forming relationships and support networks.
  • Strong support for lived-experience storytelling as one of the most effective parts of delivery, because it makes the content feel real and relatable.
W Weaknesses

What was seen as not working so well.

  • The “paper person” activity was called out as dated. The underlying purpose still makes sense, but the activity itself feels tired and may not fit the goal of helping people feel comfortable and authentic.
  • Some content is pitched too “safe” / “Switzerland” / “vanilla”, which risks missing people whose experience is more complex or higher-support-needs.
  • Parents of children with more complex / profound support needs may not see themselves in examples or discussion and can feel the content is “not my experience.”
  • Some topics, including sexuality and gender, have been treated conservatively and may need fuller, more confident coverage.
  • The slide-by-slide, highly prescriptive facilitator guide model was seen as useful for consistency, but also limiting for adult learning and flexibility.
O Opportunities

Opportunities that generated energy.

  • Shift toward more interactive and experiential learning, with less reliance on linear PowerPoint delivery.
  • Enthusiasm for UDL-style delivery, where participants can engage in different ways rather than everyone being taught identically.
  • Build activities where participants leave with something usable immediately — practical visuals, supports, or resources they can apply that day.
  • Move from slide-by-slide workbooks toward theme-based participant resources and digital facilitator guides that are easier to update over time.
  • Improve representation and intersectionality across all content, rather than treating First Nations, multicultural, and other perspectives as siloed.
T Threats & cautions

Risks raised by the team.

  • If content stays too comfortable, there is a risk of becoming less relevant to current community needs and expectations.
  • At the same time, changing so much that the project loses the consistency and safety that helps DTMs deliver confidently.
  • The project should not try to be expert in everything — sometimes the better move is to refer participants to vetted external specialist resources rather than overextending the content.
Most insightful takeaway

The strongest thread was a tension the group kept returning to: keep the strengths of consistency, shared language, and practical tools, but move away from over-scripted, overly safe delivery toward more flexible, representative, and participatory learning.
